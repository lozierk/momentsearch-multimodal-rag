## v4 · video · HIT

**Q:** What does turning the temperature up or down do to the probability distribution the model samples the next word from?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** Temperature is a constant T divided into the exponents of the softmax — a larger T flattens the distribution toward uniform (giving more weight to the lower values), while a smaller T sharpens it so the biggest values dominate, with T = 0 putting all the weight on the maximum [1].

The mechanism is described at 24:03: the softmax is used "with a constant T thrown into the denominator of those exponents. We call it the temperature, since it vaguely resembles the role of temperature in certain thermodynamics equations, and the effect is that when T is larger, you give more weight to the lower values, meaning the distribution is a little bit more uniform, and if T is smaller, then the bigger values will dominate more" [1]. The continuation at 24:30 completes the sharpening extreme: "aggressively, where in the extreme, setting T equal to zero means all of the weight goes to maximum value" [2]. You can see this flattening in the frames — at T = 4.05 the top entry only carries 0.49 of the probability [1], and at T = 7.00 the same logits give an even flatter spread with the maximum down to 0.33 and the smaller entries lifted to 0.04–0.14 [5]. Contrast this with plain softmax (no temperature), where a value meaningfully bigger than the rest dominates, e.g. 0.58 for the largest logit, so "if you were sampling from it you'd almost certainly just be picking the maximizing" element [3, 7].

Practically, this changes the text that gets generated. At temperature zero "it always goes with the most predictable word," which with the seed "once upon a time there was a" produced "a trite derivative of Goldilocks," whereas "a higher temperature gives it a chance to choose less likely words, but it comes with a risk" — the T = 5 story started more originally, about a young web artist from South Korea, but "quickly degenerates into nonsense"; the API in fact caps temperature at 2 [2, 8]. This is the same trade-off flagged earlier at 01:13: "the output tends to look a lot more natural if you allow it to select less likely words along the way at random," which is why a deterministic model gives a different answer each run [6].

## v5 · video · HIT

**Q:** In the animated walkthrough of the attention pattern grid, why are the entries below the diagonal forced to negative infinity before the softmax?

**Answer:** **Because setting them to −∞ makes softmax turn them into exactly zero while keeping each column normalized — the trick used to stop later tokens from influencing earlier ones (masking).**

At 12:12 the narration explains that you can't just zero out those entries directly: "but if you did that the columns wouldn't add up to one anymore, they wouldn't be normalized. So instead, a common way to do this is that before applying softmax, you set all of those entries to be negative infinity. If you do that, then after applying softmax, all of those get turned into zero, but the columns stay normalized. This process is called masking." The accompanying frame shows the unnormalized grid with −∞ filling everything below the diagonal, and the post-softmax grid with 0.00 in exactly those positions while the remaining entries in each column sum to one [1].

At 12:38 the purpose of the mask is stated: "even though this is more relevant during the training phase than it would be, say, running it as a chatbot or something like that, you do always apply this masking to prevent later tokens from influencing earlier ones" — i.e. the below-diagonal entries correspond to earlier words attending to later words, which must contribute nothing [2].

**Q:** What are the three RAG paradigms — Naive RAG, Advanced RAG and Modular RAG — and how do they differ?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** They are three successive stages in the evolution of RAG research — Naive RAG (a simple "Retrieve-Read" chain of indexing → retrieval → generation), Advanced RAG (the same chain-like process but with pre-retrieval and post-retrieval optimizations to fix Naive RAG's weaknesses), and Modular RAG (a flexible, reconfigurable architecture of specialized modules and rearranged pipelines) [4, 6, 9].

**The categorization itself.** The survey states that "The RAG research paradigm is continuously evolving, and we categorize it into three stages: Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, and Modular RAG, as showed in Figure 3," and that "The development of Advanced RAG and Modular RAG is a response to these specific shortcomings in Naive RAG" [9]. The paper's contribution list similarly frames it as "delineating its evolution through paradigms including naive RAG, advanced RAG, and modular RAG" [2].

**Naive RAG.** It "represents the earliest methodology, which gained prominence shortly after the widespread adoption of ChatGPT" and "follows a traditional process that includes indexing, retrieval, and generation, which is also characterized as a 'Retrieve-Read' framework" [9]. Indexing begins with cleaning and extracting raw data from formats like PDF, HTML, Word and Markdown into uniform plain text [9]; Figure 2 summarises the three steps as splitting documents into chunks encoded as vectors in a vector database, retrieving the Top k most semantically relevant chunks, and feeding question plus chunks into the LLM [9]. Its drawbacks are retrieval challenges (low precision and recall, misaligned or irrelevant chunks, missing crucial information), generation difficulties (hallucination, irrelevance, toxicity, bias), and augmentation hurdles (disjointed or incoherent outputs, redundancy, over-reliance on retrieved content, and single retrieval on the original query being insufficient for complex issues) [4].

Focusing on enhancing retrieval quality, it employs pre-retrieval and post-retrieval strategies," refining indexing through a sliding window approach, fine-grained segmentation and metadata, plus further optimizations of the retrieval process [4]. **Q:** How do query and key vectors combine to produce attention weights over a sequence?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** Each token's embedding is mapped by a query matrix and a key matrix into smaller query and key vectors; you take the dot product of every query with every key, divide by √d_k, and pass the resulting grid through a softmax to get the attention weights, which are then used as coefficients in a weighted sum of the value vectors [5, 2].

The formal recipe is written out on page 3 of the paper: attention is "mapping a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output… The output is computed as a weighted sum of the values, where the weight assigned to each value is computed by a compatibility function of the query with the corresponding key." Concretely, "We compute the dot products of the query with all keys, divide each by √dk, and apply a softmax function to obtain the weights on the values," with queries, keys and values packed into matrices Q, K, V so that Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk)V [5]. That page also notes this dot-product form is chosen over additive attention because it "is much faster and more space-efficient in practice, since it can be implemented using highly optimized matrix multiplication code," and that scaling by 1/√dk matters because for large dk "the dot products grow large in magnitude" [5].

The video explains the same expression intuitively at 10:27: "the variables q and k represent the full arrays of query and key vectors respectively, those little vectors you get by multiplying the embeddings by the query and the key matrices," and "This expression up in the numerator is a really compact way to represent the grid of all possible dot products between pairs of keys and queries" — the frame shows that grid being filled in, with keys down the rows and queries Q₁…Qₙ across the columns [2].

The query/key vectors themselves come from learned projections of the embeddings: the frame at 06:32 shows embedding E⃗₄ for "creature" being multiplied by W_Q to give a 128-dimensional query ("Any adjectives in front of me?") [4], and at 16:07 the query and key matrices are both shown as 128 × 12,288 maps, i.e. they compress the large embedding into the small shared space where the dot product is taken [6, 3].

Once the pattern is computed, the weights are applied to values rather than to keys or queries: "let's set aside all of the keys and the queries, since after you compute the attention pattern you're done with those, then you're going to take this value matrix and multiply it by every one of those embeddings to produce a sequence of value vectors… kind of associated with the corresponding keys" [9]. ## m2 · mixed · MISS

**Q:** Why does a transformer run several attention heads in parallel instead of just one?

**Answer:** **Short answer:** Because each head has its own separate key, query, and value maps, so running many in parallel lets the model capture many different ways that context can update a word's meaning at once, rather than just the single pattern one head could learn.

The mechanism is spelled out at 20:38: this is "called multi-headed attention, where you run a lot of these operations in parallel, each with its own distinct key query and value maps," with GPT-3 using 96 attention heads inside each block [6]. The key point is the *distinctness* of those maps — 96 separate sets of key/query/value matrices means 96 independently learned patterns of which tokens attend to which, all computed simultaneously.

What each of those patterns buys you is described at 05:16: attention "gives all of these lists of numbers a chance to talk to one another and refine the meanings they encode based on the context around, all done in parallel," e.g. the numbers encoding "bank" being changed by surrounding context to encode "riverbank" [10]. A single head can only enact one such kind of contextual refinement; many heads in parallel let many different relationships be picked up in the same pass.

The trade-off is parameter count. One attention head's four matrices come to "about 6.3 million parameters" [1], and "with 96 heads, each including its own variation of these four matrices, each block of multi-headed attention ends up with around 600 million parameters" [8] — so the extra expressive capacity of parallel heads is paid for directly in model size.

## m3 · mixed · MISS

**Q:** How does grounding an answer in retrieved external documents reduce hallucination compared with relying on the model's parameters alone?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** Because the generator conditions on actual retrieved passages instead of only implicit knowledge baked into its weights, its output is "more strongly grounded in real factual knowledge," which the RAG paper reports makes it "hallucinate" less and produce more factual generations than a purely parametric model [3].

**The failure mode of parameters alone.** Page 1 frames the problem: pre-trained LMs act "as a parameterized implicit knowledge base," but such models "cannot easily expand or revise their memory, can't straightforwardly provide insight into their predictions, and may produce 'hallucinations'." Hybrid models combining parametric with non-parametric (retrieval-based) memory "can address some of these issues because knowledge can be directly revised and expanded, and accessed knowledge can be inspected and interpreted" [1].

**The mechanism.** Page 1's Figure 1 overview shows how the grounding happens concretely: a query encoder plus document index retrieves the top-K documents z_i by Maximum Inner Product Search, and the seq2seq generator's prediction is marginalized over those retrieved documents, so the answer is produced conditioned on retrieved evidence rather than generated from parameters alone [1].

**Evidence that it works.** Page 3 states that people prefer RAG's generation over purely parametric BART, "finding RAG more factual and specific," and that this stronger grounding also "offers more control and interpretability," including the ability to hot-swap the retrieval index to update the model without retraining [3]. ## m4 · mixed · MISS

**Q:** How is the model's final vector turned into a probability distribution over the next token?

**Answer:** **Short answer:** the last vector is multiplied by a learned (unembedding) matrix to give one raw number — a logit — per possible token, and then a softmax (optionally with a temperature parameter) exponentiates and normalizes those numbers so they become a probability distribution over the next token.

The prediction is read off only from the **final vector** in the sequence. As stated at 03:43, "the computation you perform to produce a prediction of the next token is entirely a function of the last vector in the sequence" [8], and at 05:24 that the meaning of the passage "has somehow been baked into the very last vector," after which "we then perform a certain operation on that last vector that produces a probability distribution over all possible tokens, all possible little chunks of text that might come next" [4]. The same point is made at 06:04: "one final function is performed on the last vector in this sequence, which now has had a chance to be influenced by all the other context from the input text, as well as everything the model learned during training... the model's prediction looks like a probability for every possible next word" [6] — a probability over the tokens the input text was broken into [2].

The first step of that operation is a **matrix multiplication producing raw scores**.
