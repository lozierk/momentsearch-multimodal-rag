**Q:** What does turning the temperature up or down do to the probability distribution the model samples the next word from?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** Temperature is a constant T divided into the exponents of the softmax — raising it flattens the distribution toward uniform (giving lower-scoring words more weight), while lowering it concentrates the probability on the highest-scoring words, with T = 0 putting all the weight on the single maximum [1, 2].

The mechanic is described at 24:03: it's the softmax "with a constant T thrown into the denominator of those exponents. We call it the temperature, since it vaguely resembles the role of temperature in certain thermodynamics equations, and the effect is that when T is larger, you give more weight to the lower values, meaning the distribution is a little bit more uniform, and if T is smaller, then the bigger values will dominate more" [1]. The frame there shows T = 4.05 producing a spread-out set of probabilities across the seven entries.

At the extreme end, 24:30 continues that the bigger values dominate "aggressively, where in the extreme, setting T equal to zero means all of the weight goes to maximum value" — demonstrated by having GPT-3 continue "once upon a time there was a" at different temperatures, where "temperature zero means that it always goes with the most predictable word" while Temp = 5 picks from a nearly flat list of many candidates each at low percentages [2].

For context on the baseline behaviour without temperature, 23:35 notes that in plain softmax "if one of the numbers in the input is meaningfully bigger than the rest, then in the output the corresponding term dominates the distribution, so if you were sampling from it you'd almost certainly just be picking the maximizing" entry — which is the effect temperature either exaggerates or softens [3].

## v5 · video · HIT

**Q:** In the animated walkthrough of the attention pattern grid, why are the entries below the diagonal forced to negative infinity before the softmax?

**Answer:** **Because setting them to −∞ makes softmax turn them into exactly zero while leaving each column properly normalized** — the point being to stop later tokens from influencing earlier ones.

The walkthrough explains that you can't simply zero out the sub-diagonal entries directly: "but if you did that the columns wouldn't add up to one anymore, they wouldn't be normalized. So instead, a common way to do this is that before applying softmax, you set all of those entries to be negative infinity. If you do that, then after applying softmax, all of those get turned into zero, but the columns stay normalized. This process is called masking" [1]. The accompanying frame shows the unnormalized grid filled with −∞ below the diagonal, which becomes 0.00 in the normalized attention pattern after softmax [1].

The reason for wanting those zeros is causality: "you do always apply this masking to prevent later tokens from influencing earlier ones," and it's noted that this matters especially during training rather than, say, running the model as a chatbot [2]. It's also mentioned that some versions of attention don't apply masking at all, but the GPT example does [1].

## m1 · mixed · HIT

**Q:** How do query and key vectors combine to produce attention weights over a sequence?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** Each query vector is dot-producted with every key vector, the resulting grid of scores is divided by √d_k, and a softmax turns each column of scores into a probability distribution — the attention weights — which are then used to take a weighted sum of the value vectors [4].

The formal definition is on page 3: attention "maps a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output… The output is computed as a weighted sum of the values, where the weight assigned to each value is computed by a compatibility function of the query with the corresponding key." Concretely, "We compute the dot products of the query with all keys, divide each by √dk, and apply a softmax function to obtain the weights on the values," with queries, keys and values packed into matrices so that Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk)V [4].

Page 4 explains why the dot products are scaled: for large dk "the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients," so they are scaled by 1/√dk — the footnote notes that if the components of q and k are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product q·k has mean 0 and variance dk [7].

The video makes the same structure concrete: at 10:27 the narrator says "the variables q and k represent the full arrays of query and key vectors respectively, those little vectors you get by multiplying the embeddings by the query and the key matrices," and "This expression up in the numerator is a really compact way to represent the grid of all possible dot products between pairs of keys and queries" — the frame shows the softmax(KᵀQ/√d_k)V formula above a grid of key rows against query columns [2].

Once those weights exist, they are applied across the sequence: at 15:41 the explanation is that "you apply the same weighted sum across all of the columns in this picture, producing a sequence of changes, adding all of those changes to the corresponding embeddings, produces a full sequence of more refined embeddings popping out of the attention block," which constitutes "a single head of attention" parameterized by the query, key and value matrices shown on screen [1, 3].

Page 4 also notes this whole operation is done h times in parallel on learned linear projections of Q, K and V (multi-head attention, with h = 8 and dk = dv = dmodel/h = 64), the outputs being concatenated and projected again [7].

## m2 · mixed · HIT

**Q:** Why does a transformer run several attention heads in parallel instead of just one?

**Answer:** **Short answer:** So the model can attend to many different kinds of contextual relationships at once — each head learns its own key/query/value maps and looks at a different "representation subspace" — which a single averaged head cannot do, and because shrinking each head's dimension keeps the total cost about the same as one full-size head.

The clearest statement of the motivation is on **p. 5** of the paper (as shown in the page image): "Multi-head attention allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions. With a single attention head, averaging inhibits this." In other words, one head collapses everything into a single averaged attention pattern, washing out distinct relationships that separate heads can keep apart [10].

That page also gives the cost argument: the work employs "h = 8 parallel attention layers, or heads," and for each of these uses dk = dv = dmodel/h = 64, so that "Due to the reduced dimension of each head, the total computational cost is similar to that of single-head attention with full dimensionality" [10]. Parallel heads are therefore effectively free relative to one wide head.

The video makes the same structural point: this is "called multi-headed attention, where you run a lot of these operations in parallel, each with its own distinct key query and value maps," and notes that "GPT-3 for example uses 96 attention heads inside each block," meaning 96 distinct sets of those maps per block [6]. The separate key/query/value maps are exactly what lets each head implement a different pattern of which tokens update which.

This also explains why each individual head is kept relatively small: at **17:52** the narrator describes "constraining the overall value map to be a low rank transformation," with the four matrices summing to "about 6.3 million parameters for one attention head" [1] — a modest per-head budget that is then replicated many times over in parallel, and stacked across blocks alongside the multi-layer perceptrons mentioned at **23:11** [4].

## m3 · mixed · MISS

**Q:** How does grounding an answer in retrieved external documents reduce hallucination compared with relying on the model's parameters alone?

**Answer:** **Direct answer:** Retrieved documents act as a non‑parametric memory that the generator conditions on, so its output is tied to actual source text (e.g. Wikipedia passages) rather than to fuzzy knowledge baked into weights — which the RAG paper reports makes it "hallucinate" less and produce more factual generations [1, 3].

The core mechanism is the shift from purely implicit knowledge to hybrid memory. Page p. 1 explains that pre‑trained language models act "as a parameterized implicit knowledge base," and that this has downsides: "They cannot easily expand or revise their memory, can't straightforwardly provide insight into their predictions, and may produce 'hallucinations'." Hybrid models combining parametric memory with non‑parametric (retrieval‑based) memory "can address some of these issues because knowledge can be directly revised and expanded, and accessed knowledge can be inspected and interpreted" [1]. Architecturally, the query is encoded, top‑K documents are found by Maximum Inner Product Search, and the seq2seq generator marginalises over predictions given those different documents — so the answer is generated *from* retrieved evidence rather than from weights alone [1].

The empirical and impact claims back this up. Page p. 9 states that people "prefer RAG's generation over purely parametric BART, finding RAG more factual and specific," and in the Broader Impact section: "the fact that it is more strongly grounded in real factual knowledge (in this case Wikipedia) makes it 'hallucinate' less with generations that are more factual, and offers more control and interpretability" [3]. It also notes the retrieval index "can be hot-swapped to update the model without requiring any retraining," i.e. stale or wrong knowledge can be corrected in the index rather than re‑learned in parameters [3]. Page p. 8 corroborates that retrieval improves factual tasks specifically — fact checking, fact completion, open‑domain QA — and that a single retrieval‑based architecture generalises across them [4].

Two caveats the evidence raises. Grounding is only as good as what is retrieved: page p. 14 of the RAG survey warns that "the presence of noise or contradictory information during retrieval can detrimentally affect RAG's output quality," summarised as "Misinformation can be worse than no information at all," making noise robustness and counterfactual robustness key metrics — and it lists *faithfulness* (whether the answer is supported by the retrieved context) as an explicit generation‑quality aspect in frameworks like RAGAS, ARES and TruLens [10]. Interestingly it also reports Cuconasu et al. finding that including irrelevant documents "can unexpectedly increase accuracy by over 30%," contradicting the assumption of reduced quality [10].

## m4 · mixed · MISS

**Q:** How is the model's final vector turned into a probability distribution over the next token?

**Answer:** **Short answer:** the last vector in the sequence is multiplied by an unembedding matrix to give one raw number (logit) for every token in the vocabulary, and those numbers — which are not yet a probability distribution — are pushed through a softmax (optionally with a temperature) to turn them into normalized probabilities.

The frame at 22:41 shows exactly the first step: the final vector is hit with a big matrix whose rows produce a column of raw values (5.4, 7.1, 6.0, …) which are then shifted/scaled into numbers like −0.8, −5.0, +0.5, +1.5, +3.4, −2.3, +2.5 — and the caption over that output column reads "Not at all a probability distribution!" [3]

The second step is softmax, illustrated at 25:24: each entry is exponentiated and divided by the sum of all the exponentials, written as $e^{x_i/T}/\sum e^{x_j/T}$, converting the column $(+6.0, -5.0, +4.0, +1.5, +9.9, -2.3, +4.0)$ into the probabilities 0.19, 0.04, 0.14, 0.10, 0.33, 0.06, 0.14, shown as a bar chart. The header "softmax with temperature" and the $T = 7.00$ slider show that a temperature divides the values before exponentiating, flattening or sharpening the distribution [1].

This matches the narration that describes the operation only at a high level: "We then perform a certain operation on that last vector that produces a probability distribution over all possible tokens, all possible little chunks of text that might come next" [4], and similarly "one final function is performed on the last vector in this sequence… to produce a prediction of the next word. Again, the model's prediction looks like a probability for every possible next word" [6]. The reason it's only the last vector is that "the computation you perform to produce a prediction of the next token is entirely a function of the last vector in the sequence" [7].

Once you have that distribution, tokens are sampled rather than taken greedily: "the output tends to look a lot more natural if you allow it to select less likely words along the way at random," which is why a deterministic model "typically gives a different answer each time it's run" [9].
